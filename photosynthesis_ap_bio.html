SLIDE 4 · LIGHT REACTIONS

Light ReactionsPhotosystems I & II — the Z-scheme

  • Photosystem II (P680) — absorbs light; oxidizes water (photolysis): 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. Electrons enter ETC.
  • Electron Transport Chain (ETC) — electrons pass from PSII → plastoquinone (PQ) → cytochrome b6f → plastocyanin. Pumps H⁺ into lumen.
  • Chemiosmosis — H⁺ gradient drives ATP synthase → produces ATP. ~3 ATP per 2e⁻.
  • Photosystem I (P700) — re-energizes electrons with light; passes to ferredoxin → NADP⁺ reductase → NADPH.
  • Net output per 2 H₂O split: 3 ATP, 2 NADPH, 1 O₂ released

⭐ AP Tip
The Z-scheme is named for the shape of the electron energy diagram — electrons start low, get boosted twice by light, end at NADPH (high energy).
SLIDE 6 · CALVIN CYCLE

The Calvin CycleCarbon fixation in the stroma — 3 stages

Stage 1: Carbon Fixation

RuBisCO enzyme fixes 3 CO₂ to 3 RuBP (5C) → 6 molecules of 3-PGA (3-carbon)

RuBisCO = most abundant enzyme on Earth

Stage 2: Reduction

6 3-PGA + 6 ATP + 6 NADPH → 6 G3P (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate)

G3P = the actual sugar output; used for glucose synthesis

Stage 3: Regeneration

5 of 6 G3P + 3 ATP → regenerate 3 RuBP to continue the cycle

1 G3P exits; 2 G3P needed for glucose

Per 3 CO₂ fixed (1 turn) 3 ATP (fixation) + 6 ATP (reduction) + 3 ATP (regeneration) = 9 ATP + 6 NADPH

SLIDE 8 · PHOTORESPIRATION & C4/CAM

PhotorespirationRuBisCO's mistake — and evolution's workarounds

Photorespiration (C3 plants)

When CO₂ is low and O₂ is high (hot, dry days; stomata close), RuBisCO adds O₂ to RuBP instead of CO₂. This produces 2-phosphoglycolate (toxic; must be recycled) — wastes ATP/NADPH with no sugar output. Reduces photosynthesis efficiency by ~25%.

C4 Plants

  • Corn, sugarcane, crabgrass
  • CO₂ fixed in mesophyll by PEP carboxylase (no O₂ affinity) → 4C malate
  • Transported to bundle sheath cells → releases CO₂ for Calvin cycle
  • Kraanz anatomy separates fixation from Calvin cycle

CAM Plants

  • Cacti, succulents, pineapple
  • Open stomata at night → fix CO₂ as malate, store in vacuole
  • Stomata closed during day; release CO₂ for Calvin cycle
  • Temporal separation instead of spatial

SLIDE 11 · FACTORS AFFECTING

Limiting FactorsWhat controls the rate of photosynthesis?

☀️ Light Intensity

Increases rate until light saturation point; light-independent reactions then become limiting. Too much → photoinhibition (damages PSII).

🌡️ Temperature

Affects Calvin cycle enzymes (RuBisCO). Optimal ~25–35°C. Too high → denature enzymes. Light reactions less temperature-sensitive (physical process).

Substrate for Calvin cycle. Increases rate until CO₂ saturation. Today's atm. CO₂ (420 ppm) is often limiting for C3 plants.

Photosynthesis — Full Study Notes

AP Biology · Unit 3 · Comprehensive Lecture Notes

1. Overview & Big Picture

Concept 10.1

Photosynthesis is the process by which photoautotrophs (plants, algae, cyanobacteria) convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It is the primary source of organic carbon for nearly all life on Earth, and the ultimate source of the O₂ in our atmosphere.

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Light Energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

This equation conceals an enormous complexity — photosynthesis occurs in two main stages: the light-dependent reactions and the light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle), each in a distinct location within the chloroplast.

🔑 Key Concept
Photosynthesis is an example of energy transduction — converting electromagnetic energy (light) into chemical potential energy (glucose). The process is endergonic overall (ΔG > 0).

2. Chloroplast Structure

Concept 10.1

Chloroplasts are double-membrane organelles found in plant mesophyll cells and algae. Their internal architecture is precisely organized to maximize photosynthetic efficiency.

  • Outer membrane — freely permeable to small molecules
  • Inner membrane — selectively permeable; contains transporters
  • Intermembrane space — thin region between outer and inner membranes
  • Stroma — aqueous fluid filling the inner space; contains Calvin cycle enzymes, ribosomes, DNA (chloroplasts have their own genome)
  • Thylakoid membranes — extensive internal membrane system; photosystems, ETC proteins, and ATP synthase are embedded here
  • Grana (sing. granum) — stacks of thylakoids; increases surface area for light reactions
  • Stroma lamellae — unstacked thylakoid membranes connecting adjacent grana
  • Thylakoid lumen — space inside thylakoid membranes; H⁺ accumulates here during light reactions
📌 Structure → Function
The compartmentalization is critical: the proton gradient (H⁺) can only build up across the thylakoid membrane if the lumen is separate from the stroma. This gradient drives ATP synthesis.

3. Light Reactions

Concept 10.2 — The Z-scheme

The light reactions occur on the thylakoid membranes. They capture light energy and convert it into the chemical currency of the cell — ATP and NADPH — while releasing O₂ as a byproduct.

Photosystem II (PSII, P680): Absorbs light at ~680 nm. Two water molecules are oxidized (photolysis):
2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻
The electrons replace those lost from the reaction center chlorophyll a (P680⁺). This is where ALL O₂ is produced.

Electron Transport Chain: High-energy electrons flow from PSII through: Plastoquinone (PQ) → Cytochrome b6f complex → Plastocyanin (PC). The cytochrome b6f complex pumps additional H⁺ into the thylakoid lumen as electrons pass through, creating a proton-motive force.

Chemiosmosis (Photophosphorylation): The H⁺ concentration in the lumen (~3 pH units lower than stroma) drives H⁺ back through ATP synthase (CF₁CF₀), which phosphorylates ADP + Pᵢ → ATP. This is called photophosphorylation.

Photosystem I (PSI, P700): Electrons arriving at PSI from PC are re-energized by absorption of ~700 nm light. They pass to ferredoxin (Fd) → NADP⁺ reductase → reduce NADP⁺ + H⁺ → NADPH.

🔑 Noncyclic vs Cyclic Photophosphorylation
Noncyclic: Both PSII and PSI operate; produces ATP, NADPH, and O₂ (main pathway).
Cyclic: Only PSI operates; electrons cycle back to cytochrome b6f; produces only ATP, no NADPH and no O₂. Occurs when NADPH is in excess.

Net output of light reactions (per 2 H₂O split): 3 ATP, 2 NADPH, 1 O₂ released

4. The Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)

Concept 10.3 — Carbon fixation in the stroma

The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma and uses the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to "fix" inorganic CO₂ into organic molecules (G3P). Despite being called "light-independent," the cycle depends on ATP and NADPH which require light — so it only runs continuously in light.

Stage 1 — Carbon Fixation: RuBisCO (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) catalyzes the attachment of 3 CO₂ to 3 molecules of RuBP (5-carbon), producing 6 unstable 6-carbon intermediates that immediately split into 6 molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA).

Stage 2 — Reduction: 6 ATP and 6 NADPH are used to convert 6 3-PGA → 6 glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P). G3P is a 3-carbon sugar phosphate — the direct product of the cycle. This is where CO₂ is "reduced" using electrons from NADPH.

Stage 3 — Regeneration of RuBP: 5 of the 6 G3P molecules are used (with 3 ATP) to regenerate 3 molecules of RuBP, allowing the cycle to continue. The remaining 1 G3P is the net output.

📐 Stoichiometry
For 3 CO₂ fixed (1 turn): consumes 9 ATP and 6 NADPH; produces 1 net G3P.
For 1 glucose (6 turns): consumes 18 ATP and 12 NADPH; requires 6 CO₂.

What happens to G3P? G3P can be used to: synthesize glucose (2 G3P → glucose via gluconeogenesis), make fatty acids, amino acids, and other organic molecules — it's the building block of all cellular carbon compounds.

5. Photorespiration & C4/CAM Plants

Concept 10.4 — Adaptations

Photorespiration occurs when RuBisCO binds O₂ instead of CO₂ (when O₂ is high relative to CO₂ — e.g., hot day, closed stomata). The product (2-phosphoglycolate) cannot enter the Calvin cycle and must be metabolized in the peroxisome and mitochondria, releasing CO₂ and consuming ATP/NADPH without making sugar. This can reduce photosynthetic output by ~25%.

C4 Plants (corn, sugarcane, sorghum) evolved a biochemical CO₂ pump:

  • Mesophyll cells fix CO₂ using PEP carboxylase → oxaloacetate (4C) → malate (4C). PEP carboxylase has no affinity for O₂, avoiding photorespiration.
  • Malate is transported to bundle sheath cells (surrounding vascular bundles), where it is decarboxylated, releasing CO₂ for RuBisCO and the Calvin cycle in a high-CO₂ environment.
  • Kraanz anatomy: concentric rings of mesophyll cells around bundle sheath cells, visible in cross-section.

CAM Plants (cacti, aloe, pineapple) separate fixation and the Calvin cycle temporally:

  • Stomata open at night → CO₂ fixed to malate by PEP carboxylase → stored as malic acid in vacuoles.
  • During the day, stomata close → malate releases CO₂ → drives Calvin cycle using daytime light reactions.
  • Adaptation for arid environments: minimizes water loss while maintaining photosynthesis.

6. Pigments & Spectra

Concept 10.2 — Light absorption

Different pigments absorb different wavelengths of light. Having multiple pigments (antenna complex) allows plants to capture a broader spectrum of light energy.

  • Chlorophyll a — primary photosynthetic pigment; absorbs violet-blue (~430 nm) and red (~680 nm); reflects green; participates directly in light reactions at reaction centers P680 and P700
  • Chlorophyll b — accessory pigment; absorbs blue (~450 nm) and orange-red (~640 nm); transfers energy to chlorophyll a
  • Carotenoids (β-carotene, xanthophylls) — accessory pigments absorbing blue-green; reflect yellow/orange; also serve as photoprotective antioxidants, quenching excess light energy
📊 Absorption vs Action Spectrum
The absorption spectrum shows which wavelengths a pigment absorbs. The action spectrum shows the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength. They closely mirror each other, demonstrating that light absorption drives photosynthesis. Green light is poorly absorbed → lowest rate.

Paper Chromatography: Used to separate pigments. More soluble pigments travel further up the paper with solvent (carotenoids travel furthest; chlorophylls travel less). The Rf value = distance traveled by pigment ÷ distance traveled by solvent.

7. Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

Experimental analysis — AP Lab focus

The rate of photosynthesis is determined by whichever factor is in shortest supply (the limiting factor principle). Typical graphs show rate increasing with a factor until another factor becomes limiting.

  • Light intensity: Rate increases until the light saturation point, beyond which the Calvin cycle (not light capture) is limiting. Very high intensity can cause photoinhibition (PSII damage).
  • CO₂ concentration: Substrate for RuBisCO; higher CO₂ increases Calvin cycle rate. Elevated CO₂ may increase photosynthesis in C3 plants by suppressing photorespiration.
  • Temperature: Primarily affects enzyme-catalyzed reactions (Calvin cycle). Optimal ~25–35°C for most plants. Light reactions are less temperature sensitive. Above optimal → enzymes denature.
  • Water availability: Water is a reactant; deficiency causes stomata to close → less CO₂ entry → photorespiration increases in C3 plants.
🧪 AP Lab — Floating Leaf Disk Assay
Leaf disks infiltrated with NaHCO₃ (CO₂ source) sink initially (air removed). Under light, photosynthesis produces O₂ → disks rise. Rate measured by counting disks floating per unit time. Variables: light color, intensity, CO₂ concentration.

8. AP Exam Strategy

Exam preparation — FRQ & MCQ

High-frequency MCQ topics: Where O₂ comes from (water); which stage makes ATP vs NADPH; what RuBisCO does; distinguishing C3/C4/CAM; effects of changing CO₂/light/temp.

FRQ strategy: Use the "CRAM" framework — Claim, Reasoning, Anatomy (mechanism), and Make prediction. Always mention the specific molecule involved, the location, and the enzyme when explaining a process.

⚠️ Common Mistakes
  • Saying O₂ comes from CO₂ — it comes from H₂O!
  • Confusing PSII and PSI — PSII acts first (P680), PSI acts second (P700)
  • Saying Calvin cycle doesn't need light — it does indirectly (needs ATP/NADPH)
  • Forgetting G3P is the direct product, not glucose (must combine two G3P to make glucose)
  • Mixing up C4 (spatial) vs CAM (temporal) separation

Section I — Multiple Choice (15 Questions)
Q1 · DIFFICULTY: ★☆☆
Where in the chloroplast do the light reactions occur?
✓ The thylakoid membranes house the photosystems (PSII, PSI), the ETC, and ATP synthase. The stroma is where the Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions) occurs.
Q2 · DIFFICULTY: ★☆☆
What is the immediate source of the O₂ released during photosynthesis?
✓ Oxygen is released as a byproduct of photolysis — the splitting of water molecules at Photosystem II: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. A very common exam trap!
Q3 · DIFFICULTY: ★★☆
Which of the following correctly describes the role of NADPH in the Calvin cycle?
✓ In Stage 2 (reduction), NADPH donates electrons (reducing power) to reduce 3-PGA into G3P. ATP provides the phosphate group in this step. NADPH is oxidized back to NADP⁺.
Q4 · DIFFICULTY: ★★☆
A researcher adds a drug that blocks ATP synthase in isolated chloroplasts exposed to light. Which of the following would most likely occur?
✓ Blocking ATP synthase prevents H⁺ from flowing back into the stroma. H⁺ continues to be pumped into the lumen (by ETC), causing the gradient to build up. O₂ production would continue (PSII still works) but ATP synthesis stops, eventually halting the Calvin cycle.
Q5 · DIFFICULTY: ★★☆
Which enzyme is responsible for carbon fixation in C3 plants, and what is its key characteristic related to photorespiration?
✓ RuBisCO can bind O₂ instead of CO₂ when O₂ levels are high (hot, dry conditions with closed stomata). This leads to photorespiration, which wastes energy without producing sugar. C4 and CAM plants use PEP carboxylase (which cannot bind O₂) to avoid this.
Q6 · DIFFICULTY: ★★☆
How many turns of the Calvin cycle are required to produce one molecule of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)?
✓ Each turn fixes 1 CO₂ and produces 1 net G3P (3C). Glucose is a 6-carbon molecule, requiring 2 G3P. Since 1 net G3P per turn, you need 6 turns to fix 6 CO₂ and produce 2 G3P to combine into glucose. Total cost: 18 ATP + 12 NADPH.
Q7 · DIFFICULTY: ★★☆
In the Z-scheme, which of the following correctly describes the sequence of electron transport?
✓ The correct flow is: H₂O (photolysis) → PSII (P680) → PQ → Cytochrome b6f → PC → PSI (P700) → Ferredoxin → NADP⁺ reductase → NADPH. Electrons flow from water to NADPH, gaining energy at each photosystem.
Q8 · DIFFICULTY: ★★★
A scientist measures the rate of photosynthesis in spinach leaves at increasing CO₂ concentrations while keeping light intensity constant at a low level. The rate plateaus early. What is the most likely explanation?
✓ Even with ample CO₂, if light intensity is low, there is insufficient ATP and NADPH being produced to drive the Calvin cycle. Light becomes the limiting factor — increasing CO₂ further has no effect because the light reactions can't supply enough energy currency.
Q9 · DIFFICULTY: ★★★
Which of the following best explains why CAM plants are well adapted to desert environments?
✓ CAM plants open stomata at night (when it's cooler and more humid) to fix CO₂ as malate. During the hot, dry day, stomata close (minimizing water loss), and malate releases CO₂ for the Calvin cycle powered by daytime light reactions. Temporal separation is the key.
Q10 · DIFFICULTY: ★★★
If ¹⁸O-labeled water (H₂¹⁸O) is provided to a plant, where would you expect to find the labeled ¹⁸O atoms?
✓ Water is split at PSII (photolysis), releasing O₂ as a byproduct. Therefore, ¹⁸O from labeled water would appear in the O₂ gas released. This was confirmed by classic isotope-tracing experiments. If CO₂ were labeled with ¹⁸O, the label would end up in glucose/G3P via the Calvin cycle.
Q11 · DIFFICULTY: ★★☆
Cyclic photophosphorylation differs from noncyclic photophosphorylation in that it:
✓ Cyclic photophosphorylation involves only PSI. Electrons cycle back to cytochrome b6f after being energized, creating a proton gradient for ATP synthesis. No water is split (no PSII), and no NADPH is produced. It occurs when NADPH is in excess relative to ATP.
Q12 · DIFFICULTY: ★★★
A plant is placed in the dark but supplied with externally provided ATP and NADPH. Which of the following processes could still proceed?
✓ The Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions) does not directly require light — it requires ATP and NADPH (which normally come from light reactions). If those molecules are supplied externally, carbon fixation can proceed in the dark. This demonstrates the two stages are functionally separable.
Q13 · DIFFICULTY: ★★☆
During paper chromatography of leaf pigments, which pigment is expected to travel the furthest from the origin?
✓ β-carotene is the most nonpolar pigment, so it is most soluble in the nonpolar solvent and travels furthest. Order from top (furthest): carotenes → xanthophylls → chlorophyll a → chlorophyll b. Chlorophylls are more polar and interact more with the polar paper.
Q14 · DIFFICULTY: ★★★
Which of the following is the correct net equation for 3 turns of the Calvin cycle?
✓ For 3 turns (3 CO₂ fixed): Stage 1 uses 0 ATP/NADPH; Stage 2 uses 6 ATP + 6 NADPH (for 6 3-PGA → 6 G3P); Stage 3 uses 3 ATP (to regenerate 3 RuBP). Total: 9 ATP + 6 NADPH. Net output: 1 G3P (3C). The RuBP is regenerated internally, not shown as a "product."
Q15 · DIFFICULTY: ★★★
In a C4 plant, why is photorespiration minimized compared to C3 plants?
✓ PEP carboxylase in mesophyll cells fixes CO₂ into 4-carbon compounds (malate/OAA), which are shuttled to bundle sheath cells. There, CO₂ is released in high concentrations, flooding RuBisCO with CO₂ and making O₂ binding (photorespiration) kinetically unfavorable. Separation is spatial (2 cell types).
Section II — Free Response Questions (FRQ)

FRQ 1 — Light Reactions (8 pts)

A student places spinach chloroplasts in an illuminated buffer containing ADP, Pᵢ, and NADP⁺, but no CO₂. Describe what happens in the thylakoid membrane. Your answer must address: (a) how light energy is captured and transferred; (b) the role of the electron transport chain; (c) how ATP and NADPH are produced; and (d) what happens to the oxygen atoms from water.
Model Answer (8 points):
  • (a) Light capture (2 pts): Chlorophyll and accessory pigments in the antenna complex of PSII absorb photons (~680 nm). Resonance energy transfer moves the excitation energy to the reaction center (P680), where it excites an electron to a higher energy state. Similarly, PSI (P700) captures ~700 nm photons to re-energize electrons.
  • (b) ETC role (2 pts): Excited electrons from P680 are transferred to plastoquinone (PQ), then to the cytochrome b6f complex, then to plastocyanin (PC). This electron flow is coupled to H⁺ pumping into the thylakoid lumen, creating a proton-motive force. Electrons arrive at PSI and are excited again before being passed to ferredoxin.
  • (c) ATP and NADPH (2 pts): The H⁺ gradient drives protons back across the thylakoid membrane through ATP synthase (CF₁CF₀), which catalyzes ADP + Pᵢ → ATP (photophosphorylation). At PSI, ferredoxin passes electrons to NADP⁺ reductase, reducing NADP⁺ + H⁺ → NADPH.
  • (d) Oxygen (2 pts): Electrons removed from P680 are replenished by oxidizing water molecules at the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC) associated with PSII: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. The oxygen is released as O₂ gas (byproduct). Without CO₂, the Calvin cycle cannot proceed, but light reactions will continue as long as NADP⁺ and ADP are available.

FRQ 2 — Experimental Design (6 pts)

Design an experiment to test whether green light or red light is more effective at driving photosynthesis in aquatic plants (Elodea). Include a hypothesis, control, independent and dependent variables, and expected results with explanation.
Model Answer (6 points):
  • Hypothesis (1 pt): Red light will drive photosynthesis at a higher rate than green light because chlorophyll absorbs red light (~680 nm) strongly, whereas green light (~550 nm) is mostly reflected and transmitted.
  • Variables (2 pts): Independent variable = wavelength of light (red filter vs. green filter vs. white light control). Dependent variable = rate of photosynthesis (measured as O₂ bubble production per minute, or O₂ concentration increase). Controlled variables: light intensity (equal lumens), temperature, CO₂ concentration (NaHCO₃ in water), same Elodea species and size.
  • Control (1 pt): White light group serves as positive control; dark group serves as negative control to establish baseline O₂ production/consumption.
  • Expected Results (2 pts): Red light should show significantly more O₂ bubbles per minute than green light. This is because red light (~680 nm) matches the absorption peak of chlorophyll a and drives both PSII and PSI efficiently. Green light is poorly absorbed — most photons are reflected or transmitted, so very little energy enters the photosystems. The action spectrum of photosynthesis mirrors the absorption spectrum of the pigments.

FRQ 3 — C3 vs C4 Comparison (5 pts)

On a hot, sunny day, a farmer observes that her corn crop (C4 plant) continues to photosynthesize efficiently while her soybean crop (C3 plant) shows reduced photosynthetic rate. Explain the molecular and anatomical basis for this difference. Discuss photorespiration and how corn avoids it.
Model Answer (5 points):
  • C3 problem (1 pt): On a hot, sunny day, soybeans close their stomata to prevent water loss. This causes CO₂ levels inside the leaf to drop and O₂ levels to rise. RuBisCO (the carbon-fixing enzyme) has dual affinity for both CO₂ and O₂; when O₂ is high, RuBisCO catalyzes oxygenation of RuBP instead of carboxylation, producing 2-phosphoglycolate (a toxic compound). This photorespiration consumes ATP and NADPH without producing G3P, reducing net photosynthesis by ~25%.
  • C4 solution — biochemistry (2 pts): Corn uses PEP carboxylase in mesophyll cells to initially fix CO₂ into a 4-carbon compound (oxaloacetate → malate). PEP carboxylase has essentially no affinity for O₂, so photorespiration is avoided at the fixation step. The 4-carbon malate is then transported to bundle sheath cells, where it is decarboxylated, releasing CO₂ at high concentrations directly near RuBisCO. RuBisCO in bundle sheath cells encounters such high local CO₂ that O₂ cannot compete — photorespiration is minimized.
  • Anatomy (1 pt): Corn has Kranz anatomy — mesophyll cells arranged in a ring around bundle sheath cells (containing chloroplasts with dense RuBisCO). This spatial separation allows the CO₂ pump to function: mesophyll cells capture CO₂ efficiently and concentrate it in bundle sheath cells.
  • Outcome (1 pt): On a hot, sunny day, corn continues to fix CO₂ efficiently even with closed stomata because the CO₂ pump maintains high CO₂ at the site of RuBisCO. Soybeans lack this mechanism and suffer significant photorespiratory losses.
