Cellular Level

1. Meiosis & Genetic Variation

Meiosis ensures the transmission of traits from one generation to the next while simultaneously creating massive genetic diversity necessary for evolution.

The 3 Sources of Genetic Variation

  1. Crossing Over (Prophase I): Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) to form a tetrad. Non-sister chromatids exchange genetic material at the chiasmata. Results in recombinant chromosomes.
  2. Independent Assortment (Metaphase I): Homologous pairs line up randomly at the metaphase plate. The number of possible combinations is $2^n$ (where $n$ is the haploid number). For humans: $2^{23} \approx 8.4$ million combinations.
  3. Random Fertilization: Any sperm can fuse with any unfertilized egg ($8.4 \text{ mil} \times 8.4 \text{ mil} \approx 70$ trillion diploid combinations).

Chromosomal Abnormalities

Nondisjunction Logic Failure of homologous chromosomes (Meiosis I) or sister chromatids (Meiosis II) to separate.
  • Meiosis I Error: All gametes are abnormal ($n+1$, $n+1$, $n-1$, $n-1$).
  • Meiosis II Error: Half gametes abnormal, half normal ($n+1$, $n-1$, $n$, $n$).
Aneuploidy: Having an abnormal number of a particular chromosome (e.g., Trisomy 21 / Down Syndrome).

2. Mendelian Inheritance & Statistical Logic

Probability Rules

The Product Rule (AND)

$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$

For independent events occurring simultaneously (e.g., calculating $aabbcc$ from a trihybrid cross).

The Sum Rule (OR)

$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

For mutually exclusive events (e.g., calculating probability of a heterozygote: $Aa$ OR $aA$).

Chi-Square ($X^2$) Goodness of Fit Test

Determines if observed phenotypic ratios deviate significantly from Mendelian expected ratios.

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$$
Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): There is NO significant difference between observed and expected data. (i.e., The genes are assorting independently).

Degrees of Freedom ($df$): Number of phenotypic classes minus 1 ($n - 1$).

Evaluation: If calculated $\chi^2 > \text{Critical Value}$ (at $p=0.05$), you Reject $H_0$. This implies an alternative hypothesis: genes are linked, or evolution/selection is occurring.

3. Complex & Non-Mendelian Genetics

Epistasis

The expression of one gene modifies or masks another independently assorting gene.

Polygenic Inheritance

Multiple genes control one trait, resulting in continuous variation (a bell curve distribution). Examples: Height, skin pigmentation.

Gene Linkage & Mapping

Genes on the same chromosome inherited together. Crossing over breaks linkage.

$$\text{Recombination Frequency (RF)} = \frac{\text{Recombinants}}{\text{Total Offspring}} \times 100$$
  • $1\% \text{ RF} = 1 \text{ Map Unit (cM)}$
  • Crucial Constraint: RF cannot exceed 50%. An RF of 50% means genes assort independently.

Environmental Effects

Phenotypic Plasticity: Genotypes produce different phenotypes depending on environmental conditions (e.g., soil pH affecting Hydrangea flower color; temperature affecting Himalayan rabbit fur melanin).

Extranuclear Inheritance

Traits controlled by mitochondrial (mtDNA) or chloroplast (cpDNA). Inherited entirely maternally. An affected mother passes the trait to 100% of her offspring.

4. Pedigree Decryption Flowchart

Autosomal Recessive

  • Skips generations.
  • Unaffected parents can have an affected child ($Aa \times Aa$).

Autosomal Dominant

  • Appears in every generation.
  • Affected parents can have unaffected child ($Aa \times Aa$).

X-Linked Recessive

  • Massive male bias.
  • Affected fathers pass mutation to ALL daughters (carriers), NO sons.

Mitochondrial

  • Maternal inheritance strictly.
  • Affected mother passes to 100% of offspring.

5. Population Genetics & Hardy-Weinberg

Hardy-Weinberg Mathematical Rigor

A null hypothesis model stating allele frequencies remain constant in the absence of evolutionary influences.

/* Allele Frequencies */

p + q = 1

/* Genotype / Individual Frequencies */

p² + 2pq + q² = 1

Computational Tip: Always find $q^2$ first. The recessive phenotype frequency in the prompt IS $q^2$. Take the square root to find $q$, then calculate $p$.

The 5 Conditions for Equilibrium

If ANY of these are violated, microevolution is occurring.

  1. No mutations: The gene pool is not modified.
  2. Random mating: No sexual selection or inbreeding.
  3. No gene flow: No migration of alleles into/out of the population.
  4. Very large population size: Prevents Genetic Drift.
  5. No natural selection: All traits have equal fitness.

6. Evolutionary Forces & Modes of Selection

Non-Selective Drivers (Genetic Drift & Flow)

Genetic Drift (Bottleneck Effect)

Random fluctuations in allele frequencies. A drastic reduction in population size (disaster) leaves a random, non-representative surviving gene pool. Impacts small populations heavily.

Gene Flow & Founder Effect
  • Gene Flow: Movement of alleles between populations. Reduces genetic differences between populations over time.
  • Founder Effect: A few individuals isolate to form a new colony. The new gene pool does not match the original population (a form of drift).

Modes of Natural Selection

Natural Selection acts on the phenotype (improving fitness), which consequently alters genotype frequencies over time.

Directional Selection

Favors one extreme. Bell curve shifts.
Ex: Antibiotic resistance.

Stabilizing Selection

Favors intermediate variants. Curve narrows.
Ex: Human birth weights.

Disruptive Selection

Favors both extremes. Curve splits.
Ex: Small & large finch beaks.

7. Speciation & Isolating Mechanisms

Biological Species Concept: A species is a group of populations whose members have the potential to interbreed in nature and produce viable, fertile offspring.

Allopatric Speciation

Gene flow is interrupted when a population is divided into geographically isolated subpopulations (e.g., river changes course, mountain range forms). Mutations and selection occur independently in the separated populations.

Sympatric Speciation

Speciation occurs in populations that live in the same geographic area. Often driven by polyploidy (common in plants), habitat differentiation, or sexual selection.

8. Phylogeny, Cladistics & Evidence

Evidence for Evolution

  • Fossil Record: Transitional fossils show evolutionary links (e.g., Tiktaalik). Radiometric dating establishes timelines.
  • Homologous Structures: Anatomical resemblances representing variations on a structural theme present in a common ancestor (e.g., forelimbs of mammals). Shows divergent evolution.
  • Analogous Structures: Similar function, different origin (e.g., bird wing vs. insect wing). Shows convergent evolution.
  • Vestigial Structures: Remnants of features that served important functions in an organism's ancestors (e.g., whale pelvis).
  • Molecular Biology: The most precise evidence. Universal genetic code (DNA/RNA). Comparing amino acid sequences in conserved proteins (like Cytochrome C) reveals relatedness.

Reading a Cladogram

Principle of Maximum Parsimony: The cladogram that requires the fewest evolutionary events (appearances of shared derived characters) is the most likely.

Outgroup: The Lancelet. Used as a baseline for comparison. Diverged before the lineage containing the groups being studied.
