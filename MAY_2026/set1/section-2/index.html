Background
Epidermal growth factor (EGF) binds the EGF receptor (EGFR), a receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK), on the surface of epithelial cells. EGF binding causes EGFR dimerisation and autophosphorylation of tyrosine residues on its cytoplasmic domain, activating the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade. Activated ERK enters the nucleus and drives transcription of proliferation genes including c-Myc and cyclin D1. In approximately 30% of colorectal cancers, the KRAS proto-oncogene carries the G12D point mutation (glycine→aspartate), which disables the GTPase activity of the RAS protein. Scientists studied two cell lines: normal colon epithelial cells (NCE) and KRAS G12D mutant cancer cells (KRC). Both were treated with increasing concentrations of gefitinib, a competitive inhibitor of the EGFR kinase domain. Cell viability (%) and pERK/total-ERK ratios were measured (Figures 1–3, Table 1).

Model Answer — Question 1 9 points available
Part (a) — 2 points 1+1
Point 1 (TM domain): The transmembrane domain of EGFR consists of a single α-helix composed predominantly of nonpolar (hydrophobic) amino acids whose R-groups are compatible with the hydrophobic interior of the phospholipid bilayer. Because the bilayer core repels polar and charged species, a hydrophobic helix is thermodynamically stable within it.

Point 2 (phosphorylation): Upon EGF binding, EGFR dimerises and one kinase domain trans-phosphorylates tyrosine residues on the other subunit's cytoplasmic domain. These phosphotyrosines serve as docking sites for adaptor proteins containing SH2 domains (e.g., Grb2). Without phosphorylation, the cytoplasmic domain presents no binding sites for these adaptor proteins → no downstream complex can assemble → RAS is not activated → the cascade cannot propagate.

Part (d) — 2 points 1+1
Prediction: Yes, the combination would reduce KRC viability more effectively than gefitinib alone.

Justification: Gefitinib alone fails in KRC cells because mutant KRAS bypasses EGFR and constitutively activates RAF→MEK→ERK (Figure 3). Trametinib directly blocks MEK, which is downstream of mutant KRAS and therefore inaccessible to gefitinib. Combining both drugs inhibits the pathway at two independent nodes — EGFR (gefitinib) and MEK (trametinib) — preventing any residual pathway activity. With MEK blocked, even constitutively active KRAS cannot generate pERK → c-Myc and cyclin D1 are not transcribed → KRC cells stop proliferating → viability decreases. This vertical pathway inhibition strategy ("combo therapy") is the clinical rationale used in KRAS-mutant cancers.

Part (e) — 1 point 1
Normally, RAS is activated (GDP→GTP exchange) when EGFR is stimulated, then rapidly inactivated by its intrinsic GTPase activity (GTP→GDP hydrolysis), terminating the signal. The KRAS G12D substitution (glycine→aspartate at position 12) sterically blocks the GTPase catalytic site — RAS cannot hydrolyse GTP and is permanently locked in the active GTP-bound conformation. Constitutively active RAS continuously activates RAF, which phosphorylates MEK, which phosphorylates ERK — the entire cascade runs indefinitely without any EGF ligand, driving uncontrolled cell proliferation.

Background
CRISPR-Cas9 is a bacterial immune system adapted as a precision gene-editing tool. A single guide RNA (sgRNA) with a sequence complementary to a target DNA strand directs the Cas9 nuclease to create a double-strand break (DSB). DSBs are repaired by either (1) Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ), an error-prone pathway producing small insertions/deletions (indels), or (2) Homology-Directed Repair (HDR), which uses a donor DNA template to insert a precise sequence. Scientists used CRISPR-Cas9 to delete three base pairs from the smc3 gene encoding the SMC3 cohesin subunit. Three cell lines were generated: wild-type (WT), heterozygous edited (smc3+/Δ), and homozygous edited (smc3Δ/Δ). Chromosome segregation errors during mitosis were quantified (Table 2). A separate experiment measured NHEJ and HDR efficiencies at increasing sgRNA concentrations (Figure 4).

Model Answer — Question 2 9 points available
Part (a) — 1 pt 1
The sgRNA contains a 20-nucleotide spacer sequence that is complementary to the target DNA strand of smc3. Sequence recognition occurs via Watson-Crick complementary base pairing (A–T and G–C hydrogen bonds) between the sgRNA spacer and the target strand. This positions Cas9's two nuclease domains (HNH and RuvC) to cleave both DNA strands, creating a blunt-ended DSB three bases upstream of the PAM sequence (NGG). Specificity depends entirely on the 20-nucleotide complementarity between sgRNA and target.

(b) Significance: WT error rate 2.1 ± 0.4 → ±2SE range: 1.3–2.9%. smc3+/Δ rate 14.7 ± 1.9 → ±2SE range: 10.9–18.5%. These ranges do NOT overlap → the difference is statistically significant. (c) Cohesin dosage: Cohesin (SMC3) physically holds sister chromatids together from S phase until anaphase. At 48% SMC3, most sister-chromatid pairs are still joined; at 3% SMC3 nearly no cohesin is present → sister chromatids are not held together → kinetochores cannot biorient → spindle assembly checkpoint is overwhelmed → massive segregation errors (81.2%) and aneuploidy (44.1%).

(d) sgRNA/NHEJ relationship: Positive relationship — NHEJ efficiency increases as sgRNA concentration rises (3% → 52% from 0–100 nM) because more Cas9-sgRNA complex means more DSBs, and NHEJ repairs any DSB. HDR remains low (≤6%) because HDR requires a donor DNA template physically present at the break site. Increasing Cas9 activity (more breaks) does not provide more template; HDR is limited by template availability, not by cutting efficiency. HDR is also cell-cycle restricted (active only in S/G2 phase).

(e) Gene therapy: Both must be co-delivered because Cas9-sgRNA creates the break at the precise smc3 location, and the donor template must be present at that moment and location so HDR machinery can use it for precise repair. Without the template, NHEJ would occur, potentially worsening the mutation. Corrected cells (both alleles repaired) would produce 100% normal SMC3 → segregation errors return to ~2% WT baseline, dramatically lower than smc3+/Δ's 14.7%.

(f) 3-bp vs 1/2-bp deletion: A 3-bp deletion removes exactly one codon; the reading frame is preserved because 3 is a multiple of the 3-nucleotide codon unit. All downstream codons continue to be read in-frame → protein is missing one amino acid but may retain partial function. A 1-bp or 2-bp deletion shifts the reading frame (frameshift) → all downstream codons are misread → aberrant amino acid sequence → premature stop codon → truncated non-functional protein → complete loss of function.

Model Answer — Question 3 4 points
A 1
Black locust invasion dramatically increases nitrogen fixation (N₂ → NH₄⁺) via its Rhizobium root symbionts, raising NH₄⁺ from 1.2 to 8.7 mg/kg — a 7.25× increase. This elevated NH₄⁺ also accelerates nitrification (NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ by nitrifying bacteria), raising NO₃⁻ from 0.4 to 6.3 mg/kg (15.75×). The net effect is that both forms of plant-available nitrogen increase massively, fundamentally altering the nutrient dynamics of the invaded ecosystem.

B 1
Within surface populations of the same river, OCA2 amino acid identity is 99.8% — virtually no change. Between surface and cave populations (diverged ~120,000–150,000 years ago), OCA2 identity is only 94.2–94.9%, meaning cave populations have accumulated ~5.1–5.8% amino acid substitutions not found in surface fish. This measurable divergence in protein sequence reflects allele frequency change (evolution) occurring in cave populations since geographic isolation.
C 1
In permanent darkness, eyes provide no survival benefit — they cannot detect light — while melanin synthesis consumes metabolic energy and cellular resources. Individuals with loss-of-function mutations in oca2 or eye development genes have slightly higher fitness because they redirect that energy to reproduction, foraging, or other functions. Natural selection therefore consistently and repeatedly favours the loss-of-function allele (p) in every cave population facing the same selective pressure (perpetual darkness + energy cost), driving p toward fixation independently in all cave systems.
D 1
Genetic drift is random — it is equally likely to increase or decrease any allele frequency by chance. If eye loss were due to drift, some cave populations would by chance lose the functional allele while others retained it, producing variable outcomes across 30 populations. Instead, all ~30 cave populations have independently evolved the same phenotype (eye loss and albinism) in the same direction. This consistent, repeated, directional outcome in every independently evolving population is the hallmark of natural selection, not drift. Only selection — acting on the same fitness cost in the same environment — would produce the same adaptive outcome reproducibly across 30 independent trials.

Background
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are ~22-nt non-coding RNAs that repress gene expression post-transcriptionally. The cardiac miRNA miR-208 is upregulated in hypoxic cardiac muscle cells. miR-208 targets the 3′UTR of THRAP1 mRNA, which encodes a transcriptional repressor of cardiac myosin heavy chain (β-MHC) genes. When THRAP1 is present, β-MHC transcription is repressed; when THRAP1 is absent, β-MHC is expressed, enabling forceful cardiac contraction. Figure 6 shows miR-208 levels and THRAP1 protein under normal and hypoxic (stressed) conditions.
Figure 6. miR-208 expression and THRAP1 protein level in normal vs hypoxic cardiac cells
A.Describe how miR-208 uses the RISC complex to reduce THRAP1 protein levels. Specify where in the cell this occurs and the molecular basis of target recognition. (1 pt)
B.Using Figure 6, explain the inverse relationship between miR-208 and THRAP1 in hypoxic cells. Then predict β-MHC transcription in hypoxic cells relative to normal. (1 pt)
C.A mutation destroys miR-208's binding site in THRAP1's 3′UTR. Predict the effect on β-MHC expression and cardiac function in hypoxic cells. (1 pt)
D.Explain why miRNA-mediated THRAP1 repression is fundamentally different from silencing the THRAP1 gene by DNA methylation of its promoter, even though both reduce THRAP1 protein. (1 pt)
Model Answer — Question 5 4 points
A 1
miR-208 is loaded into the RISC complex in the cytoplasm. The guide strand of miR-208 base-pairs with a complementary sequence in the 3′UTR of THRAP1 mRNA via Watson-Crick hydrogen bonds — sequence-specific target recognition. Because animal miRNA-mRNA pairing is often imperfect, RISC primarily causes translational repression (ribosome stalling) and deadenylation of THRAP1 mRNA, reducing THRAP1 protein output. This is post-transcriptional regulation — the THRAP1 gene continues to be transcribed, but the protein is not efficiently translated.
B 1
In hypoxic cells, miR-208 rises ~3.8× (1.0 → 3.8), causing increased RISC-mediated translational repression of THRAP1 mRNA → THRAP1 protein falls ~10× (2.9 → 0.3). Without THRAP1, the transcriptional repressor of β-MHC is absent → β-MHC gene is derepressed → β-MHC transcription is higher in hypoxic cells than in normal cells. This allows the stressed heart to upregulate its contractile protein as an adaptive response to reduced oxygen.
C 1
If the miR-208 binding site is mutated, miR-208 cannot bind THRAP1 mRNA even when miR-208 is highly expressed in hypoxic cells. THRAP1 mRNA is no longer repressed → THRAP1 protein remains high during hypoxia → THRAP1 continues to repress the β-MHC promoter → β-MHC expression stays low → cardiac muscle cannot upregulate contractile protein in response to stress → impaired adaptive response to hypoxia, reduced cardiac contractile force.
D 1
miRNA regulation is post-transcriptional — the THRAP1 gene is still transcribed into mRNA; regulation acts on the mRNA in the cytoplasm after transcription is complete. DNA methylation of the THRAP1 promoter is transcriptional/epigenetic regulation — it prevents RNA polymerase from binding, so THRAP1 mRNA is never made. Key distinction: miRNA affects mRNA translation (cytoplasm, mRNA level); promoter methylation affects transcription initiation (nucleus, DNA level). Additionally, miRNA regulation is rapidly reversible (miR-208 levels change within hours); promoter methylation is inherited through cell divisions and is less rapidly reversible.

Background
Shugoshin (SGO1) protects centromeric cohesin during meiosis I by recruiting PP2A phosphatase, which dephosphorylates and shields centromeric cohesin from separase cleavage. Scientists generated four sgo1 point-mutation alleles (sgo1-A through sgo1-D) plus a heterozygous deletion (sgo1-D = +/del) in yeast. All four heterozygous mutant strains produce ~50% of WT SGO1 protein. Premature sister chromatid separation (PSCS) during meiosis I was measured (Figure 7).

Model Answer — Question 6 4 points
A 1
sgo1-B is the most severe allele — its PSCS frequency is ~36%, compared to sgo1-D's ~5%. The difference is approximately 31 percentage points; the ±2SE error bars for sgo1-B (36 ± ~12%) and sgo1-D (5 ± ~6%) do not overlap, confirming a statistically significant difference.
B 1
Because all alleles produce the same amount of protein (~50% WT), protein quantity cannot explain sgo1-B's high PSCS. The sgo1-B amino acid substitution must specifically disrupt SGO1's functional domain — most likely the region that recruits PP2A phosphatase or the centromere-binding domain — rendering the protein non-functional for cohesin protection even though it is produced normally. This demonstrates that protein quantity ≠ protein function: the sgo1-B protein is a qualitative loss-of-function (non-functional protein present at normal levels), not a quantitative one.
C 1
sgo1-D (heterozygous deletion) produces ~50% WT SGO1 protein from its single WT allele (the deleted allele contributes nothing). Panel B shows sgo1-D has only ~5% PSCS — not significantly different from WT's ~3% (overlapping ±2SE error bars). This demonstrates that 50% of normal SGO1 protein is sufficient for normal centromeric cohesin protection; the function is not haploinsufficient. The cohesin protection pathway operates normally at half the protein level.
D 1
Prediction: Some haploid cells will have 2 copies of a chromosome (n+1), while their sister cells will have 0 copies (n−1), producing aneuploid gametes. Justification: When centromeric cohesin is lost prematurely in meiosis I, sister chromatids separate before anaphase II. They then segregate independently and randomly, so some cells receive both chromatids of a chromosome pair while others receive none. Aneuploid gametes are problematic for fertility because fertilisation produces a trisomic zygote (e.g., trisomy 21) or a monosomic zygote (e.g., monosomy X) — most of which are either developmentally non-viable or cause serious chromosomal disorders, resulting in implantation failure, spontaneous abortion, or severe developmental disability.
